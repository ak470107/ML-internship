Which content pages should you refresh first?

By Ummema Atif — FinTech, FAST-NUCES Lahore (Batch 28) · ML Internship Track, FlyRank AI, mentored by Haris · Repo · Portfolio
Abstract

Which of FlyRank's declining content pages should a manager review first? Working from a 30,000-page, 32-client slice of a ~79-million-row production SEO warehouse (Jan 2025–Jun 2026), I trained a Logistic Regression classifier to rank pages by decline risk, validated on a client-grouped holdout so no client's pages ever appear in both training and test data. The model reaches 0.65–0.72 precision in the top 20–50 ranked pages against a 51% base rate — modest but real, and provably honest: the identical model scored under a naive random split reports an inflated precision@20 of 1.00 by quietly letting 31 of 32 clients leak across the split. The output ships as a reason-coded, human-reviewed action queue (25% of pages flagged) rather than an autonomous system, with an explicit no-go list for what this model must never decide alone. This is decision support for a content manager's weekly triage, not a verdict on any page or client.

01

Introduction

A content manager at FlyRank scans a dashboard every week trying to decide which of thousands of pages deserve a rewrite, a refresh, or nothing at all. Guessing wrong costs editorial hours either way: reviewing a page that didn't need it wastes time; missing one that did means a real decline goes unaddressed. In the 30,000-page slice studied here, 54% of pages are already trending down — too many to review by hand, and too many for a coin flip to sort well.

The obvious fix — a simple rule like "flag anything stale with traffic" — sounds reasonable, but the signals that separate declining from non-declining pages overlap heavily page-to-page (shown below). There is no clean threshold. That overlap is exactly the case a model that weighs several signals together is built for, and exactly where "we built a model" can quietly stop meaning anything unless the model is validated the way this one was: honestly, on data it has genuinely never seen.

02

Data

The release is the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse, spanning roughly 79 million rows in its primary daily-performance table alone (one row = one content item, for one client, on one calendar day), across 104 clients and ~520,000 content items, from 27 Jan 2025 through 30 Jun 2026. The final month ships separately as a sealed sample, held out from label-building on purpose so it can later serve as a genuinely blind check.

This paper works from a 30,000-row, 32-client starter slice, already rolled up from the daily warehouse grain to one row per content page.

Excluded, on purpose

  • The label's own source — the columns that define "declining" can never also be a feature.
  • Six last-30-day / previous-30-day traffic columns — the label's own numerator and denominator; confirmed as leakage below.
  • Client and content IDs — pseudonyms, used only to group the train/test split, never as model inputs.
  • Rows with no confirmed data availability flag — a zero there means "no data," not "no engagement."

All identifiers in this dataset ship pre-pseudonymized by FlyRank. No client names, raw queries, or URLs appear anywhere in this paper or its underlying repo.

03

Methodology

Label. A page is declining if its impressions fell 20%+ comparing a trailing 30-day window against the prior 30 days — an observed, measured label, not a manually assigned tag.

Why not a simple rule? Comparing declining vs. non-declining pages, CTR and engagement rate shift in the expected direction on average — but their 25th/50th/75th percentiles overlap heavily. No single cutoff cleanly separates the two groups, which is why this is a ranking problem, not an if-statement.

Features. 24 numeric and 8 categorical signals: search volume, competition, CPC, on-page size, 90-day traffic and engagement metrics, content age, and tier labels for freshness, word count, position, and impressions. Full exclusion list in Data, above.

Baseline. A transparent rule — flag a page if it's 91–180 days stale and still has meaningful visibility — using only two fields any content manager already sees on a dashboard, no ML at all.

Model. Logistic Regression, compared against a Random Forest, both trained and evaluated on an identical client-grouped 80/20 split: no client appears in both train and test, because pages from the same client can share a CMS template or writer — a random row split would let the model partly memorize that instead of learning a general refresh signal.

Same model. Same data. Same random seed. Only the split design changes — and the "perfect" 1.00 precision@20 turns out to be measuring client memorization, not skill.

Leakage checks. The six last-30d/prev-30d columns were deliberately added back into the "clean" feature set as an attack test: ROC-AUC jumped from 0.583 to 0.848, confirming they had to be excluded — and that the exclusion wasn't over-caution.

05

Limitations & honest framing

What this work cannot claim
  • Modest, honestly-measured skill. ROC-AUC ~0.58 on the honest split — real but modest lift over chance. This model earns a rank-ordering role, not an autonomous-decision one.
  • Cross-sectional, not causal. One snapshot per page, no repeated observations over time. A predicted decline-risk score is an observed pattern match, not proof that any specific action reverses a page's trajectory.
  • Thin buckets exist. Two of the four freshness tiers hold only ~175 rows each (vs. 20,480 and 9,171 for the other two) — reason codes leaning on those tiers stand on a much smaller evidence base.
  • No time-aware validation. The split here is client-grouped, never time-based; this dataset has no repeated per-page observations over time, so seasonal drift or the model's shelf life going forward is untested, not just unmeasured.
  • Scoped to a 30k-row starter slice, not the full ~79M-row warehouse — client-level patterns here should not be read as verdicts on any specific client's real account.
  • Importance is not direction. Content age ranks top-3 in feature importance, but its own univariate relationship runs opposite a tidy "older pages decay more" story (declining pages average 236 days old vs. 280 for stable ones, r = −0.164) — a feature can matter to a model without matching a simple narrative.

Ranked recommendations

Every page is scored by a model retrained on the full 30,000-row slice, then grouped into an archetype → action map:

ArchetypeConditionReason codeAction
Declining & Visible — Click Laggardtop-quartile risk + visible + good position + CTR below its tier's meanctr_fix_opportunityOptimize title/meta
Declining & Visible — Needs Refreshtop-quartile risk + visiblestale_needs_refreshRefresh content
Declining but Quiettop-quartile risk + low visibilitylow_visibility_monitorMonitor only
Stableeverything elsenot_flaggedNo action

Priority within flagged rows = predicted risk × clicks_90d × cpc — a defensible click-equivalent-value proxy (never impressions × CPC, since impressions aren't billable).

Human review is mandatory

Before acting on any flagged row: check whether several top-priority pages share one client (a shared technical issue, not N independent opportunities — 8 of the top 20 rows in this run came from a single client), confirm the page isn't already mid-edit, and treat the risk score as a rank, not a per-page probability.

Must NOT be automated
  • No auto-published content changes — a human editor approves every edit.
  • No automatic deprioritization based on a "not flagged" label.
  • No feeding this queue's own picks back in as next month's training label — that would let the model quietly grade its own homework.
  • No cross-client comparison, performance-review, or budget-allocation use.
  • No claim that a flagged page's metrics will improve if refreshed.

Monitoring

Re-score monthly. Watch base rate (reference: 54%), mean predicted risk (reference: ~0.50), precision@50 on a fresh holdout (reference: 0.72), and per-client concentration in the top of the queue — a retrain or re-review is warranted if any drift materially from these reference values.
